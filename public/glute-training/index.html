Why the glutes

One muscle group, five Pillar 1 categories

Few muscle groups touch as many parts of Exercise & Mobility. Training them is a direct route to the things you want to keep doing.

P1-C1

Joint health

Gluteus medius and minimus help steady the hip joint and share load that would otherwise reach the knee and low back.

P1-C2

Functional independence

Standing from a chair, climbing stairs and getting up from the floor are glute-driven tasks.

P1-C3

Mobility

A strong, flexible hip lets you take a full stride. Training through a comfortable full range keeps that range useful.

P1-C4

Strength

The gluteus maximus is among the largest, most powerful muscles in the body, and the main hip extensor.

P1-C5

Endurance

Your hip muscles control the pelvis on every step. Endurance here keeps a long walk steady.

The core idea

The rule of thirds

Instead of repeating one exercise, each session includes one movement from each of four categories. Together they cover the three directions the hip works in: vertical, horizontal and lateral.

Train three days a week, vary the choice each time, and the hip muscles are loaded from every angle. Heavier moves cost more recovery; sideways work costs very little, which is why the source calls it “penalty-free” volume.

What the evidence supports

Ten principles, graded honestly

We label every principle so you can weigh it. Several rest on coaching experience more than controlled trials.

How we reframed the source material. The notes behind this guide were written for general gym-goers who want to build glute muscle, and they say plainly that they contain nothing specific to people over 45. We kept the principles and added three guardrails for active aging: start light, use support for single-leg work, and offer spine-conscious swaps for hinges and deep squats.

Measure what matters

The 30-second chair stand

A simple, widely used check of lower-body strength. Sit in a standard chair, arms crossed over your chest, and count how many full stands you complete in 30 seconds.

Retest every four weeks. A rise of one or two stands is a real, meaningful change.

Reference values: CDC STEADI age and sex bands (age 60 and over). A screening guide, not a diagnosis.

Common questions

Quick answers

How often should I train my glutes?

The source program trains the glutes three days a week, with one exercise from each of four categories in every session. That is a reasonable approach for most healthy adults; start with two days if you are new and add a third once recovery feels easy.

Aren’t squats enough?

Squats work the gluteus maximus well, but the gluteus medius and minimus get far less from squats and thrusts. Adding direct sideways work, ideally at a few different angles, covers the gap.

How deep should I squat?

Deeper squats may build more glute muscle than shallow ones, but going past parallel appears to add little. Choose a depth that is comfortable for your hips and knees. A box or chair is a fine way to set it.

How do I know if I’m doing too much?

Look for excessive soreness and a dip in performance. When you feel worn down, drop to one or two sets or switch to lighter band work. The readiness check on the program page turns this into a simple decision.
