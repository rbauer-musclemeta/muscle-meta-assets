Power check

Power is how quickly your legs can produce force, the quick push behind standing from a chair. This ten-minute check estimates it from a 30-second chair-stand test, so you can see where you are and track change over time.

What you need

  • A sturdy armless chair placed against a wall (and a tape measure for its seat height)
  • A timer, and someone nearby for safety
  • Your body mass and height

This is a screening estimate for education. It is not a diagnosis or medical advice. Your answers are processed in this page and are not stored or sent anywhere.

Step 3 of 4 · The 30-second test

Chair-stand test

  1. Sit in the middle of the chair, back upright, feet flat, arms crossed over your chest.
  2. Practice once or twice, resting 30 to 60 seconds in between.
  3. On "go," stand fully upright and sit back down so you touch the seat, as fast as you safely can, for 30 seconds.
  4. Count only complete stands. You may stop at any time if you feel unwell.

Use the same chair and the same way of testing each time you repeat this.

Promising Cross-sectional studies in European cohorts. Power here is an equation-based estimate, not a direct force measurement.

Things worth discussing with a clinician

    Can power change?

    In a randomized trial of 112 healthy older adults (average age 69), 8 to 12 weeks of explosive-intent resistance training raised measured peak power by about 14 to 15%, compared with 3% in controls (de Vos 2005, PMID 15972618). That trial measured power only. It did not test falls or independence. Whether fast or heavy work suits you depends on your health, so ask a physical therapist or qualified exercise professional.

    How this was calculated

    Power (W) = body mass × 0.9 × 9.81 × (0.5 × height − chair height) ÷ [(30 ÷ stands) × 0.5]. It treats the body as rising a distance equal to half your height minus the seat height, moving 90% of body mass, with half of each stand spent rising (Alcazar et al., Sci Rep 2020, PMID 33004970). Relative power is absolute power ÷ body mass.

    Reference points: below 2.6 W/kg (men) or 2.1 W/kg (women) was linked with mobility limitation in about 9,300 adults aged 60 and over (Alcazar et al., J Cachexia Sarcopenia Muscle 2021, PMID 34216098). A later Spanish cohort of 1,475 found 2.53 and 2.01 W/kg (PMID 39790033). Smallest meaningful change: 0.42 W/kg (men) and 0.33 W/kg (women). These come from European community-dwelling older adults. People with recent surgery, illness or immobilization were excluded from the validation study, so treat your result with extra care if that is you. Different sit-to-stand protocols are not interchangeable.
